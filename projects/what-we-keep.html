Essay / 2026

God
Is A Woman

Published in: Arohana · Read time: 10 min

we do not demand the sea to choose which of its properties holds its true character. why then must a woman?

I have been thinking about how little room we allow a woman to change. A woman is kind, and kindness becomes her name. she is patient, and patience becomes her obligation. She listens, forgives, and accommodates. The qualities through which we have come to know her begin to acquire the force of a contract. We expect her to remain legible to us. We want to be able to predict the temperature of her heart. Then, one day, she is angry. How quickly the vocabulary changes! The kind woman becomes cruel. The patient woman becomes difficult. The woman who has spent years absorbing the discomfort of others becomes wild. when her kindness was evidence of her character, her anger became evidence against it. So did we ever know her at all? We seem to mistake the part of a woman we have encountered for the totality of her being. We meet her in one season and resent her for showing up in another. We remember her as water in a glass and are offended when she becomes a flood. But can you contain water? You can put it in a vessel. You can measure it, carry it, drink from it. You can build your life around its capacity to sustain you. And still, water can drown you. It can dissolve the ground beneath your feet, enter the smallest fracture in a wall, leave a city unrecognizable. The substance has not betrayed its nature. It has just revealed another part of it. So why do we insist that a woman must be understood through her most useful qualities? There is a difference between knowing a woman and knowing what to expect from her. We have confused the two. I keep returning to the image of water because it refuses the moral categories we impose upon women. Water is neither virtuous nor wicked. Its capacity to nourish does not cancel its capacity to destroy. We do not demand the sea to choose which of its properties holds its true character. Why then must a woman? I want to think about the feminine as a force that cannot be exhausted by a description, or a catalogue of qualities like gentleness, fertility, beauty, and care. Not even power. I am interested in something more elemental, which is the capacity to become, to alter, to bring forth, and to dissolve. The feminine in this sense, is not a synonym for women. It is a way of thinking about existence itself. In Hindu Shakta traditions, Sakti is divine power. the dynamic potency through which the universe manifests and transforms. In some of these traditions, the Goddess is not subordinate to a masculine creator. She is the supreme reality. She is not simply the possessor of power. She is power!!! Kali is worshipped with the imagery of death upon her body. Severed heads, a garland of skulls, weapons, and a tongue extended beyond the boundaries of conventional beauty. She is associated with time, destruction, and liberation. She is not a decorative expression of femininity. I think about how differently we encounter anger in a goddess and anger in a woman. Kali’s terrifying form can be approached through ritual, philosophy, devotion. A living woman who frightens us is more likely to be asked to explain herself. We can contemplate destruction when it belongs to the divine, but become uncomfortable when a woman destroys the life that has been arranged for her. We can admire the goddess who stands outside convention while asking the woman beside us to behave. It seems easier to worship a woman’s power when it cannot make a demand upon us. Kali is NOT a convenient metaphor for female anger. She is a goddess with deep esoterism that exceeds any contemporary interpretation. But her presence makes me question why the sacred is permitted to contain qualities that we consider unbecoming in a woman. Why must a woman be gentle to be good? Why must she be forgiving to be compassionate? Why is her capacity to endure so often celebrated, while her capacity to decide that she has endured enough is treated as a failure? I think of the sea again. Its violence is not proof that its stillness was a lie. This is what disturbs us about a woman’s changing nature. We want her to be a stable object in our lives, a familiar shore against which we can measure ourselves. We do not always know what to do with a person who contains more than one truth. We prefer the portrait to the living body. But a portrait does not change. A woman does. There is another kind of power in Circe, the Greek sorceress who transforms Odysseus’s companions into pigs. Her knowledge is embodied in action. She changes what other people believe to be fixed. The men arrive at her island with assumptions about the world and discover that their bodies are not beyond her influence. The sorceress has always seemed to me an interesting figure in the history of how women are imagined. She knows things. She possesses techniques, secrets, a relationship with the world that does not depend upon the authority of the men who encounter her. Her knowledge has consequences. We have often made a monster of the woman whose knowledge cannot be comfortably assimilated into the existing order. We call her manipulative when she understands the terms of an arrangement. We call her dangerous when she can alter it. We become suspicious of her intelligence when it ceases to serve our purposes. Circe is not Kali, and Greek mythology cannot be collapsed into Hindu theology. But both figures disturb the assumption that a woman must remain passive in order to remain recognisably feminine. Each possesses a power that changes the conditions under which other beings exist. We are accustomed to thinking of identity as something that must be preserved. We ask who a person really is, as though beneath the changes there must be one final unchanging description waiting to be discovered. But identity is not a buried object. It is also a process, something that happens through the accumulation and abandonment of forms. A woman can be kind and furious. She can want to be held and want to be left alone. She can love someone and recognise that she cannot continue loving them in the same way. These are not necessarily contradictions that need to be resolved. They may be the conditions of being a person whose inner life is not organised for the convenience of an observer. What would it mean to allow a woman to remain incompletely known? I have been thinking about creation, too, and about the womb. The womb is a portal of creation. I do not mean this as an instruction about what a woman’s body is for. Not every woman can or wants to give birth, and no woman’s creative life should be measured against her reproductive capacity. I mean the womb as a material fact that opens onto a philosophical question- what is the nature of that interval in which something is becoming, but has not yet materialized? Before a body enters the world, it develops beyond the world’s gaze. Before a thought becomes language, it may exist as an intuition without a name. Before a painting is visible, the artist lives with an image that cannot yet be separated from the uncertainty of its making. Creation has an interior life. We celebrate what has emerged because it can be seen, named, and judged. We are less comfortable with the period before emergence, when a thing is changing without providing evidence of what it will become. The unfinished work is difficult to evaluate. The life in transition is difficult to categorize. We are tempted to call the absence of a final form an absence of substance. But something can be real before it is visible. This is why I find the womb such a compelling image. It makes me think of creation as a state of becoming rather than a single act of production. Something is held, nourished, and transformed. Something develops according to a process that cannot be entirely commanded from outside. The same is true of art, though the analogy has its limits. A novel can gestate for years. An idea can alter the person who is trying to understand it. A woman can spend a long time becoming someone her former life has no language for. There is a particular cruelty in asking a person to explain what she is becoming before she has finished becoming it. We want declarations. We want a plan, a reason, a finished account of the self. We ask a woman why she has changed as though change were a departure from the truth, rather than one of the ways in which truth becomes apparent. The feminine as creative power suggests another possibility. Creation does not always proceed by imposing a fixed design upon passive matter. Sometimes it requires receptivity, duration, AND the capacity to remain with uncertainty. Sometimes the work must be allowed to develop beyond the reach of the maker’s original intentions. And yet creation requires stillness, too. I have been using the word nothingness, although I do not mean emptiness. I mean a fullness that has not yet become comprehensible to us. The silence before a sound. The dark in which the eye has not yet distinguished a shape. The blank canvas, which is not a deficiency, but the condition in which every possible mark can still be made. In some Shaiva and Shakta understandings, Siva and Sakti are considered in relation to consciousness and power, stillness and dynamism. Their relationship is interpreted differently across traditions, and I do not want to reduce these complex theological ideas to a simple opposition between men and women. What interests me is the philosophical possibility of a reality in which stillness and movement are distinguishable, yet inseparable. Without the canvas, there is nowhere for the painting to appear. Without the movement of the hand, the canvas remains unmarked. Stillness makes creation possible, while movement gives possibility a form. Neither is merely the opposite of the other. I think we sometimes mistake stillness for emptiness because we are accustomed to measuring existence by what it produces. But the unmanifest is not necessarily the nonexistent. The absence of a visible form does not mean the absence of potential. There are states of being that precede our ability to understand them. And this is where the question of God becomes personal. I do not want a goddess who is feminine because she is beautiful, or maternal because she gives life, or benevolent because she makes herself useful. I want to contemplate a divinity whose existence is not exhausted by a single function. A divinity that can contain creation and destruction without requiring one to cancel the other. I want to think about what it means to be a woman without being obliged to represent womanhood correctly. The woman who is kind must be allowed to become angry. The woman who is strong must be allowed to collapse. The woman who creates must be allowed to stop. The woman who has spent her life nourishing others must be allowed to discover that she too has a hunger that cannot be satisfied by giving. None of these changes makes her previous self fraudulent. None of them reveals a hidden, more authentic woman who was waiting to emerge and replace the old one. They are all part of the life she is living. We have confused consistency with integrity. We want people to remain recognisable because recognisability reassures us. I return to the image of the womb, to the dark interior in which something becomes possible before it becomes visible. I return to Kali, whose divinity does not depend upon our comfort, and Circe, whose knowledge changes the world she inhabits. I return to Shakti, as a way of thinking about the power through which existence moves. A woman has every season inside her. I think about this when i think about the blood. every month, the body opens a door it has opened before. The lining gathers, thickens, breaks down, and leaves. The body begins again. Even when nothing has been conceived, something has been built and dismantled. There is a particular kind of violence in this repetition, and a particular kind of tenderness. The body does not ask whether it has the energy to begin again. It simply moves through its own weather. I wish women were not reduced to the season in which they were found. We are taught to love women in spring. When they are generous, when they are beautiful in ways that reassure us, when their tenderness makes life easier for everyone around them. we are less prepared for winter. For the woman who has nothing left to give, who closes the door, who cannot make a home out of herself for another person. We call her cold, we call her difficult, and we forget that winter belongs to the same earth that gave us flowers. I know what it is to be tired of giving. To wonder how much of myself i can offer before the offering becomes an expectation. To love people and still resent the version of myself that love seems to require. There is a particular shame in this exhaustion. When you have built an identity around being understanding, kind, accommodating, you begin to experience your own limits as a moral failure. you feel guilty for having reached the end of what you can give. and so you give a little more. i wonder how many women have sat alone in a room, furious with themselves for feeling resentful toward the very people they love. how many have mistaken exhaustion for a lack of love, when perhaps they have simply been loving without enough room left for themselves. I am tired of loving in ways that require me to disappear. So what am i worth when i am not giving anything to anyone? what am i worth when i am not being chosen, when i am not being useful, when i am not making money, when i have no impressive title to place beside my name? What am i worth when the work i do is invisible, when the care i give leaves no receipt, when the things i make have not yet found an audience? What am i worth when i am simply a woman sitting in a room with herself, unable to point to anything that proves she deserves to be there? I have learned how easily the language of worth becomes the language of exchange. We ask what a woman brings to the table, as though she must arrive carrying something that justifies the space she occupies. beauty, money, intelligence, domesticity, ambition, emotional maturity. The inventory changes according to the person doing the measuring. There is always another quality to acquire, another deficiency to correct, another reason to postpone the moment in which we might finally consider ourselves enough. I think i have mistaken the desire to be loved for the desire to be found worthy. I have spent so much time trying to become a woman who is easy to love that i have neglected the woman who exists when nobody is looking. i do not want to romanticise exhaustion. I do not want to call suffering sacred simply because women have suffered for so long. there is nothing inherently divine about being depleted. A woman should not have to bleed, labour, forgive, nurture, create, and begin again just to earn the right to be called powerful. So for me, divinity is not in how much she can withstand. it is in the fact that she CAN stop. The earth does not apologise for the forest fire because the forest fire destroys. The ocean does not make itself smaller because its waves frighten someone standing on the shore. Nature contains both the conditions of life and the conditions of its undoing. We understand this when we look at the landscape. Why do we struggle to understand it when we look at a woman? I still catch myself measuring my life against things that can be counted. The work i have done, the money I have made, the recognition I have received, the names people use when they introduce me. I know these things matter. i want to make a living from what i create. I want to be recognised for the work of my mind. I want to build something that is mine. I do not want to pretend that material independence is unimportant simply because I am trying to question the terms by which we measure human worth. but i want to know whether these things can belong to me without becoming the conditions of my existence. Can I want a name without believing I am nothing until i have made one? Can I want to make money without treating every unproductive day as evidence against my life? Can i want to be loved without turning myself into a continuous argument for why i should be? I have mistaken my becoming for a debt I must repay. I want to make a life, to have a name, to create things that outlive a passing glance. But i no longer want these things to serve as evidence that I deserved to exist. want to belong to myself, even when I have nothing to show for it. even when I am unfinished, uncertain, and especially unremarkable to the world. If we can imagine a god who creates, destroys, and begins again, why is it so difficult to accept a woman who does the same? I am not waiting to bloom to call myself alive.
