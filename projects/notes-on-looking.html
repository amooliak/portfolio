Essay 2025

An Inheritance
Of Words

Published in: After5pm Read time: 6 min

Language is not only how we speak. It is how we feel.

Long before we learn to name our emotions, we inherit the sounds that will one day hold them. The first coos we hear, the lullabies sung into our skin, the soft scoldings, the kitchen talk, the overheard whispers. All of these shape the architecture of feeling before we can even think. The mother tongue is not just the first language. It is the first container. It holds the world in the way a mother holds her child: fully, bodily, with touch, tone and scent. Every other language we learn after it is a kind of distance from that original warmth. I’ve been doing crossword puzzles in Telugu lately, and something strange has been happening. I’m not just solving clues. I’m recovering sensations. A word appears and with it, an image, a memory. Telugu doesn’t allow me to skim. I cannot glide over meaning the way I often do in English. Each word roots me, makes me pause, and insists I feel. In Telugu, there is no room for vagueness. Even the most abstract of concepts must take form. You can’t just say “time” and expect the word to stand alone, floating. Instead, you evoke the turning of the moon, he gradual wilting of a jasmine garland left overnight on a windowsill, or the way a lamp flickers when oil runs low. Time must be pictured, not merely passed. Sorrow too, doesn’t arrive as just a feeling. It comes as an image. A cracked pot leaking water. A well gone dry. The smell of rain in the air that never falls. These are not just metaphors, they are the language. In Telugu, you don’t describe sadness in theory. You describe the moment it sat beside you, quiet and heavy, like a broken window on a windy day. Everything!!! Grief, hope, longing, love. It is all embodied. Spoken through images that the body can recognize before the brain can name. This is what makes Telugu, and perhaps many mother tongues, feel so intimate: you don’t speak about life, you see it as you speak. You feel it more fully, because the language insists you do. This is not coincidence. This is the psyche speaking in its true language: images. The psyche has no words of its own. Only impressions. It is the brain that translates those impressions into language. But the kind of language we use determines what gets translated and what gets lost. When I speak in Telugu, I feel more. Not because I am more emotional, but because the language demands it. It expects you to feel, before it lets you speak. It will not let you say “pain” without asking you what it looked like, where it hurt, what season it arrived in. English, by contrast, allows me to explain my feelings without really entering them. This makes me wonder: What happens to a life when it is lived entirely in a second language? What happens when the language you predominantly speak is not the one your body first knew? To change the language is to change the filter through which the world is absorbed. It is to rearrange the furniture of the mind. What you notice, what you suppress, what you emphasize, what you grieve. All of it is shaped by the tongue that forms your thoughts. English, for me, is efficient. Clever. Capable of abstraction. But Telugu? Telugu is emotional memory. It is breath-heavy. Image-heavy. It doesn’t describe the world. It evokes it. Perhaps this is why returning to your mother tongue, even briefly, feels like stepping into warm water. You recognize the temperature immediately, even if you didn’t know you were cold. And so I ask: What if we all spoke the language that made us feel the most? What if our thoughts were sculpted not for clarity alone, but for closeness? What if, instead of mastering words, we let words master us? Draw us back into the body, memory, AND meaning? Because when the tongue changes, so does the soul. Not permanently, but perceptibly. A shift in sound becomes a shift in sense. And before you know it, the life you’re living is not quite the life you would have lived, had you said it differently.
